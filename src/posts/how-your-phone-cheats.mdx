<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading intro">
<p class="eyebrow">Dissecting a photograph</p>
<h2 id="the-photo">What your camera truly sees</h2>

Photography is one of my biggest hobbies, its one of my favorite ways to experiment a new place when traveling. And I always come back to thinking about how amazing phone photos are with such constrained hardware. We have all had a few phones on our lives, and over the years I'm sure you've noticed their photos going from <b>pretty bad</b> to <b>surprisingly good</b>. Good enough that most people never think about buying a camera anymore.

The incredible part is that the jump in quality didn't come from hardware mainly, <b>it came from software</b>! In this post, I wanted to explore how software is able to make such a difference in the final image and show you the tricks behind it.

To do so, we will look at a photo taken at sunset with a Nexus 6P (yeah, feeling old?). I picked this one because Google later published the <b>raw data</b> from the sensor as part of a research dataset, so we have both things to compare side by side: a <b>finished photo</b> and <b>what truly the camera "saw"</b>.

</div>

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">Everything between these two pictures is software.</p>
<p>To get from one to the other, the phone does <b>three different cheats</b>, and each cheat makes the photo a bit better. Let's follow them in order to see how they change the photo.</p>
</div>
</section>

<p>First things first: why is that picture <b>gray</b>?</p>

<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow">Cheat 1 / Color and Neighboring pixels</p>
<h2 id="cheat-one">Your camera is color-blind.</h2>
<p>First things first: <b>why the camera "sees" gray?</b> The why, it comes down to how a camera sensor is built, and it has two parts.</p>
</div>

<Sensor />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">Each pixel is just one number for one color.</p>
<p>So the camera is color-blind, up to a point. It only sees <b>how much light there is</b>, and the filters are what give each number a color. The sensor in the Nexus phone has {megapixels} million of these pixels, with <b>twice as many <b class="c-g">green</b> ones</b> as <b class="c-r">red</b> or <b class="c-b">blue</b>, because our eyes are most sensitive to green. This is due to how they are laid out on the sensor, with a pattern of squares of <b class="c-g">Green</b>, <b class="c-b">Blue</b>, <b class="c-g">Green</b>, <b class="c-r">Red</b> pixels. That layout is called a <b>Bayer pattern</b>, after Bryce Bayer, who came up with it at Kodak in the seventies. Almost every camera still uses it, the big ones too.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>A photo needs <b>three colors</b> in every pixel, but the sensor only measured <b>one</b>.</p>
</section>

<Agenda at={1} />

<section class="beat cont" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow slide-only">Cheat 1 / Color and Neighboring pixels</p>
<h2 id="a-third-of-a-photo">A third of a photo</h2>
<p>But here is the problem with how the sensor is laid out, what a photo needs is <b>three colors in every pixel</b>, but the sensor <b>only measured one</b>. That leaves the phone with <b>a third of a photo</b>, so the first cheat is that it kinda <b>guesses the rest</b>. Lets see it happen in a small patch of our photo.</p>
</div>

<Mosaic />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">Two thirds of the color in this photo was never measured.</p>
<p>The guess we use on this page is the simplest one: <b>the average of the closest pixels</b> that did measure that color. Real phones use smarter guesses that follow edges, but the idea is the same. Engineers call this step <b>demosaicing</b>, in case you want to look it up.</p>
<p>And this isn't special to our photo. <b>Nearly every photo any phone takes</b> works like this. It works because <b>neighboring pixels usually look alike</b>, and it fails in small ways where they don't. If you have ever seen odd colored ripples on a striped shirt or a distant fence, that was this guess going wrong.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Great, we have color! Now look at it <b>up close</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow">Cheat 2 / Neighboring moments</p>
<h2 id="cheat-two">Color and its dreaded enemy: Noise</h2>
<p>Guessing gave us color, and from far away the picture already looks kinda like a photo. But if we look at it <b>up close</b>, it doesn't look that great.</p>
</div>

<Noise />

<div class="reading">
<p>And the phone <b>made it worse on purpose</b>. Every time a camera takes a photo, it <b>collects light for a short moment</b>. A longer moment lets more light in, which is what dark things need, but it's too much for bright ones. To keep the sunset from turning white, the phone collected light for <b>only 1/{shutter}&nbsp;s</b>, so almost no light from the rocks reached the sensor. We can brighten them afterwards, but that <b>brightens the noise too</b>. It's the same reason night photos from older phones looked like they were sprinkled with sand.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Noise is <b>random</b> but the scene is <b>constant</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Take the same photo again: the rocks come out <b>the same</b>, the noise comes out <b>different</b>.</p>
</section>

<Agenda at={2} />

<section class="beat cont" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow slide-only">Cheat 2 / Neighboring moments</p>
<h2 id="eight-photos">You never took one photo.</h2>
<p>The good news is that there's one thing we can use against noise: <b>it's randomness</b>. Take the same photo again and the rocks come out <b>the same</b>, since the scene is constant, but the noise comes out <b>different</b>. So if we had several photos of the same moment, we could tell which part is which. And we do have them, because the phone didn't take one photo. <b>It took {frames}.</b></p>
</div>

<Burst />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">One press of the button. {frames} photos, lined up and averaged.</p>
<p>There's a catch, though: <b>each extra photo helps less than the one before</b>. Averaging cuts the noise by <b>the square root of the number of photos</b>, so it takes four photos to halve it and sixteen to halve it again. Drag the slider above and you'll see it. Going from one photo to two is a big jump, and going from seven to eight is hard to notice.</p>
<p>In 2015 the burst started when you pressed the button. As far as I know, newer phones go one step further: they keep the latest frames in memory while the camera app is open, so part of your photo can come from <b>before you pressed anything</b>.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Now the picture is clean. One thing left to decide: <b>how bright</b> should it be?</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>A camera collects light for <b>a moment</b>. A longer moment, <b>more light</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>To save the <b>sky</b>, the phone picked a short one: 1/{shutter}&nbsp;s. The <b>rocks</b> got almost no light.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>We can brighten the picture afterwards, but <b>one amount</b> has to work for both.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow">Cheat 3 / Area by area</p>
<h2 id="cheat-three">One brightness doesn't fit this scene.</h2>
<p>After the first two cheats we have clean color in every pixel. There's one thing left to decide, and it's <b>how bright the picture should be</b>. Remember that the phone collected light for a very short moment to save the sky, so the rocks came out dark. We can brighten the picture afterwards, but <b>one amount of brightening</b> has to work for both the sky and the rocks. Try it yourself with the slider.</p>
</div>

<Tradeoff />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">The sunset or the rocks. Never both.</p>
<p><b>Every camera has this problem</b>. When a scene has a lot of contrast, we lose detail at one end. Go for the dark parts and the bright ones burn out. Protect the bright parts and the dark ones go black. Photographers call the distance between the darkest and the brightest thing in a scene its <b>dynamic range</b>, and a sunset has a lot of it.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>So the phone doesn't pick. It gives <b>each area its own brightness</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>It makes a <b>brighter copy</b> of the picture, then picks, area by area, <b>which copy looks better</b>.</p>
</section>

<Agenda at={3} />

<section class="beat cont" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow slide-only">Cheat 3 / Area by area</p>
<h2 id="region-by-region">The rocks were never this bright.</h2>
<p>So the phone doesn't pick. It gives <b>each area of the picture its own brightness</b>, and it does it <b>in software, after the photo is taken</b>. If you have ever wondered what the HDR in your camera app stands for, this is it: <b>high dynamic range</b>.</p>
</div>

<Exposure />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">The first two cheats recover what was there.<br /> This one decides what we see.</p>
<p>Nothing in the sensor data asked for that ×{times(data.gain.groundEv)} on the ground. Someone wrote software that <b>decided what a good sunset photo looks like</b> (a colorful sky and rocks we can actually see), and the phone edited this one to match.</p>
<p>In 2015 the rule for where to brighten was simple: prefer pixels that are <b>neither too dark nor too bright</b>. How much to brighten was, literally, <b>a matter of taste</b>. The team labeled about <b>5,000 scenes by hand</b> with the result they liked best, and the phone looked up the ones most similar to ours. Today's phones go further. They recognize skies and faces and treat each one differently, and portrait mode blurs backgrounds that a lens this small can barely blur at all.</p>
</div>
</section>
