<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading intro">
<p class="eyebrow">A photograph, taken apart</p>
<h2 id="the-photo">This is not what the camera saw</h2>
<p>Photography is one of my hobbies, and there's something about it I've been curious about for a while. We have all had a few phones by now, and we have all watched their photos go from pretty bad to surprisingly good. Good enough that most people never think about buying a camera.</p>
<p>The funny part is that the camera inside a phone is still tiny. Most of that jump came from software, and we never get to see what that software does, because the phone only shows us the finished picture.</p>
<p>This photo is an exception. Someone took it at sunset with a Nexus 6P, and Google later published it together with the raw data from the sensor, as part of a research dataset. So we have both things side by side: the finished photo and what the sensor measured.</p>
</div>

<Reveal />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">Everything between these two pictures is software.</p>
<p>To get from one to the other, the phone cheats three times, and each cheat goes a bit further than the one before. Let's follow them in order on this one photo.</p>
<p class="present-hint">I first gave this as a ten-minute lightning talk. Press <kbd>P</kbd> to see it as slides, then use the arrow keys. Or just keep scrolling.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Between those two pictures, the phone <b>cheats three times</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>First things first: why is that picture <b>gray</b>?</p>
</section>

<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow">Cheat 1 / Neighboring pixels</p>
<h2 id="cheat-one">Your sensor is color-blind.</h2>
<p>First things first: why is the sensor's picture gray? It comes down to how a sensor is built, and it has two parts.</p>
</div>

<Sensor />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">One pixel, one number, one color.</p>
<p>So the camera is color-blind, up to a point. It only sees how much light there is, and the filters are what give each number a color. The sensor in this phone has {megapixels} million of these pixels, with twice as many green ones as red or blue, because our eyes are most sensitive to green.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>A photo needs <b>three colors</b> in every pixel. The sensor only measured <b>one</b>.</p>
</section>

<Agenda at={1} />

<section class="beat cont" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<h2 id="a-third-of-a-photo">A third of a photo</h2>
<p>And here is the problem. A photo needs three colors in every pixel, and the sensor only measured one. So the first cheat is that the phone guesses the other two. We can watch it happen on a small piece of our photo.</p>
</div>

<Mosaic />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">Two thirds of the color in this photo was never measured.</p>
<p>The guess we use on this page is the simplest one: the average of the closest pixels that did measure that color. Real phones use smarter guesses that follow edges, but the idea is the same. Engineers call this step demosaicing, in case you want to look it up.</p>
<p>And this isn't special to our photo. Nearly every photo any phone takes works like this. It works because neighboring pixels usually look alike, and it fails in small ways where they don't: fine fabric, distant fences, thin text.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Great, we have color! Now look at it <b>up close</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow">Cheat 2 / Neighboring moments</p>
<h2 id="cheat-two">The color is there. So is the noise.</h2>
<p>Guessing gave us color. But if we look at the result up close, it doesn't look that great.</p>
</div>

<Noise />

<div class="reading">
<p>And the phone made it worse on purpose. To keep the sunset from turning white, it opened the shutter for only 1/{shutter}&nbsp;s, so almost no light from the rocks reached the sensor. We can brighten them afterwards, but that brightens the noise too.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Noise is <b>random</b>. The scene is <b>not</b>.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Take the same photo again: the rocks come out <b>the same</b>, the noise comes out <b>different</b>.</p>
</section>

<Agenda at={2} />

<section class="beat cont" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<h2 id="eight-photos">You never took one photo.</h2>
<p>The good news is that there's one thing we can use against noise: it's random, and the scene isn't. Take the same photo again and the rocks come out the same, but the noise comes out different. So the phone didn't take one photo. It took {frames}.</p>
</div>

<Burst />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">One press of the button. {frames} photos, lined up and averaged.</p>
<p>In 2015 the burst started when you pressed the button. As far as I know, newer phones go one step further: they keep the latest frames in memory while the camera app is open, so part of your photo can come from before you pressed anything.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>Now the picture is clean. One thing left to decide: <b>how bright</b> should it be?</p>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>This scene has a <b>bright sky</b> and <b>dark rocks</b>, and a camera has to pick <b>one brightness</b> for both.</p>
</section>

<section class="beat" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<p class="eyebrow">Cheat 3 / Area by area</p>
<h2 id="cheat-three">One brightness doesn't fit this scene.</h2>
<p>After the first two cheats we have clean color in every pixel. There's one thing left to decide, and it's how bright the picture should be. The problem is that this scene has a bright sky and dark rocks, and a camera has to pick one brightness for both.</p>
</div>

<Tradeoff />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">The sunset or the rocks. Never both.</p>
<p>Every camera has this problem. When a scene has a lot of contrast, we lose detail at one end. Go for the dark parts and the bright ones burn out. Protect the bright parts and the dark ones go black.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="beat say" data-beat aria-hidden="true">
<p>So the phone doesn't pick. It gives <b>each area its own brightness</b>.</p>
</section>

<Agenda at={3} />

<section class="beat cont" data-beat>
<div class="reading">
<h2 id="region-by-region">The rocks were never this bright.</h2>
<p>So the phone doesn't pick. It gives each area of the picture its own brightness, and it does it in software, after the photo is taken.</p>
</div>

<Exposure />

<div class="reading">
<p class="thesis">The first two cheats recover what was there.<br /> This one decides what we see.</p>
<p>Nothing in the sensor data asked for that ×{times(data.gain.groundEv)} on the ground. Someone wrote software that decided what a good sunset photo looks like (a colorful sky and rocks we can actually see), and the phone edited this one to match.</p>
<p>In 2015 the rule for where to brighten was simple: prefer pixels that are neither too dark nor too bright. How much to brighten was, literally, a matter of taste. The team labeled about 5,000 scenes by hand with the result they liked best, and the phone looked up the ones most similar to ours. Today's phones go further. They recognize skies and faces and treat each one differently, and portrait mode blurs backgrounds that a lens this small can barely blur at all.</p>
</div>
</section>

<div class="reading source-note">
<h2 id="sources">The photograph and the fine print</h2>
<p>Photograph and derived images: <a href="https://www.hdrplusdata.org/dataset.html">HDR+ Burst Photography Dataset</a>, Hasinoff et al. (2016), <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>. Burst <code>{data.burst}</code>, a Nexus 6P, {frames} frames at ISO {data.frames[0].iso}. "What the phone showed" is the dataset's own HDR+ result. Everything else is a small teaching pipeline of ours, not a reproduction of Google's processing.</p>
<details>
<summary>How each picture was made</summary>
<ul>
<li>The raw sensor view averages every {data.bin} × {data.bin} block of sensor pixels, with no color, white balance, lens correction or brightening.</li>
<li>The shoreline window is {data.crop.width} × {data.crop.height} sensor sites starting at ({data.crop.x}, {data.crop.y}) in frame {data.frames[0].name}, the frame the dataset uses as its reference. We brighten it ×{data.viewGain} with a soft shoulder so the shadows are visible. Every picture of the window uses that same rendering.</li>
<li>The sensor drawing is a schematic. A real pixel also has a tiny lens on top and wiring around it.</li>
<li>Color uses bilinear demosaicing, the dataset's lens-shading map, its white balance and its color matrix. "Which filter" shows each site's own measurement in its own color channel and nothing else.</li>
<li>Alignment is a single shift per frame for this window, found by comparing brightness at three scales and refined to an eighth of a pixel. A whole-frame merge needs more than one shift, because the camera also rotates.</li>
<li>The pixel we follow through the burst is at ({data.pixel.x}, {data.pixel.y}) in the window, in the flattest patch we could find, read after alignment. The chart shows its brightness in each frame relative to the average of the {frames}.</li>
<li>"Grain" is the strength of pixel-to-pixel variation in the green channel, relative to one frame. It mixes noise with real fine detail, so it understates the improvement. Perfect averaging of {frames} frames would reach {Math.round(100 / Math.sqrt(frames))}%.</li>
<li>Nothing in this scene moves. A merge that rejects samples disagreeing with the reference frame discards {data.robust.rejected} of {data.robust.total.toLocaleString('en-US')}, so we left that step out of the story.</li>
<li>Our blend renders the reference frame at ×1 and ×{2 ** data.fuse.longEv}, scores each pixel of both by how close its brightness is to a middle gray, blurs that choice, and mixes the two. HDR+ also fuses two renderings of one merged frame, but it blends them scale by scale, which avoids the glow our single blurred map leaves along the ridge.</li>
<li>The brightening map divides the brightness of the phone's result by the brightness of our dark (×1) version of the reference frame, in blocks of 48 × 48 sites, then smooths it. The figures quoted are medians over the top fifth and the bottom two fifths of the frame.</li>
<li>Single frames and the filter mosaic are stored without loss, because lossy compression blurs color at the scale of one pixel. Merged and whole-frame images are WebP at quality 88 to 90.</li>
</ul>
</details>
